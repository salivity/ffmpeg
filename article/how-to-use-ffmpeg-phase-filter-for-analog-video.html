How to Use FFmpeg Phase Filter for Analog Video

Analog video captures from sources like VHS, Betamax, or LaserDisc frequently suffer from field phase shifts where interlaced fields become misaligned across frame boundaries. This article explains how field phase shifts occur, how the FFmpeg phase video filter operates, and the exact commands required to automatically or manually correct field alignment to eliminate unwanted combing artifacts.

Understanding Phase Shifts in Analog Video

Analog video is inherently interlaced, consisting of alternating top and bottom fields captured sequentially in time. When analog video is digitized, capture cards assemble these pairs of fields into digital frames. If the capture device drops a single field, begins recording on the wrong field, or encounters bad timecode sync, the temporal pairing breaks.

In a phase-shifted capture, the top field of the current frame is paired with the bottom field of the subsequent frame. On progressive source material transferred to analog tape (such as films), this causes severe horizontal comb lines (interlacing artifacts) across the entire image during motion, even when the underlying footage should be progressive.

The FFmpeg phase Filter

The FFmpeg phase filter realigns delayed or phase-shifted fields without having to deinterlace or discard spatial resolution. It inspects adjacent fields and shifts the pairing forward or backward to reconstruct the original frames.

The filter accepts a primary mode parameter that dictates how field correction is resolved:

  • a (Auto): Automatically analyzes the fields to detect whether a phase shift is present frame-by-frame, selecting the pairing that produces the least combing.
  • t (Top-field first): Forces correction by pairing the current bottom field with the top field of the next frame.
  • b (Bottom-field first): Forces correction by pairing the current top field with the bottom field of the next frame.
  • p (Progressive): Treats the input as progressive and does not alter field ordering.

Correcting Phase Shifts with FFmpeg Commands

1. Automatic Phase Correction

For variable captures or tapes where the phase shift occurs intermittently due to tracking errors or edits, automatic detection is the most effective choice.

ffmpeg -i input.mkv -vf "phase=a" -c:v libx264 -crf 18 -preset slow -c:a copy output.mkv

In this command, phase=a continuously monitors the image lines. When it detects motion combed with an out-of-phase field, it dynamically shifts the field pairing to restore clean frames.

2. Fixed Manual Correction

If an entire capture has a constant phase shift from start to finish, manually forcing the field shift prevents detection errors during slow-moving or low-contrast scenes.

If the top field is delayed:

ffmpeg -i input.mkv -vf "phase=t" -c:v libx264 -crf 18 -preset slow -c:a copy output.mkv

If the bottom field is delayed:

ffmpeg -i input.mkv -vf "phase=b" -c:v libx264 -crf 18 -preset slow -c:a copy output.mkv

Order of Operations in Filter Chains

For analog restorations, the phase filter must be applied before any spatial scaling, deinterlacing, or color grading. Rescaling an interlaced frame before correcting the phase shift permanently blends the out-of-phase scan lines, making clean field separation impossible.

If working with telecined film material on analog tape, use the phase filter prior to inverse telecine (fieldmatch and decimate):

ffmpeg -i input.mkv -vf "phase=a,fieldmatch,decimate" -c:v libx264 -crf 18 -c:a copy output.mkv

This sequence first repairs any broken field phase, provides cleanly matched full frames to fieldmatch, and drops redundant duplicate frames with decimate to produce a pristine, full-resolution progressive file.
